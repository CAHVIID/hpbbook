# Box building systems for type houses

:::{warning} Under revision
This chapter is being reworked and may change substantially.
:::

Many new single-family houses in Denmark are type houses: houses sold from a catalogue, with a fixed floor plan and a few variants. A typical type house has about 145–210 m², 1 storey and often a flat or single-sided roof.

This chapter develops type houses built from room-sized 3D volumes (boxes) made in a factory. Using 3D volumes is faster and potentially cheaper, but only if the floor plan is catered towards 3D volumes. Also external and internal dimensions are constrained by the need for truck transport, however, combinations of 3D volumes and 2D elements can be used to bypass transport requirements. The design challenges are to:
- build **functional, internal spaces using primarily 3D volumes**, and 2D elements only when forced to. A key element is to decide and place the assembly interfaces.
- plan for acquiring **sufficient levels of insulation of walls, ceilings and floors.** The insulation may be integrated into the 3D volume from factory or added as a 2D element on site. A key element is to decide on these assembly interfaces.
- plan for good **daylight levels** with sized facade windows, skylights and light tunnels
- early planning of **installation routing**, from the perspectives of factory production, assembly process, and afterwards. Installations entail ventilation ducting, hot water, cold water and sewage. Hydronic heating should not be relevant given the magnitude of heating demand.
- plan for efficient **ventilative cooling** using hatches, automatic skylights and airflow pathways underpinned by stack effect.

## E1.2 Internal dimensions

The largest piece of furniture in a house is the double bed, 1.8 × 2.0 m. With about 1 m of free space around the bed and a 0.6 m wardrobe, a bedroom needs an internal width of 3.8–3.9 m ({numref}`fig-bedroom-dimensions`, {numref}`fig-bedroom-plans`). All other rooms fit within this width.

## E1.3 External width

A box is carried as special transport, with a permit from the Danish Road Directorate and escort vehicles {cite:p}`vd-saertransport2023`. In practice, the external width of a box is limited to 4.5 m ({numref}`fig-box-width`).

A facade wall with U < 0.08 W/(m²·K) needs λ/U = 0.037/0.08 = 0.46 m of mineral wool, or 0.5–0.6 m including studs, boards and cladding. Made with the box, it leaves 3.8–3.9 m for the room. Made as a separate 2D element mounted on site, it leaves 4.3 m, at the cost of an extra element, an extra lift and an airtight joint made on site.

## E1.4 External height and insulation

The truck and the box together may be at most 4.1 m high. A low-loader leaves 3.3 m for the box, a special low-loader 3.8 m ({numref}`fig-box-height`).

:::{figure} figures/e1/box-height.*
:label: fig-box-height
:alt: Four stacked bars from the road up to a dashed line at 4.1 m. Each bar has a grey loader, a brown floor, a white 2.50 m room and a hatched roof. Low-loader with 0.30 floor: loader 0.8, floor 0.30, roof 0.50. Solution 1: loader 0.8, floor 0.10, roof 0.70. Solution 2: loader 0.8, floor 0.30, roof 0.15, with a dashed separate roof element above labelled added on site. Special low-loader: loader 0.3, floor 0.30, roof 1.00.
:width: 100%

Four ways to share the 4.1 m height between truck, floor, room and roof. Dimensions in m.
:::

A roof needs 0.45–0.5 m of insulation plus room for structure, fall and ventilation ducts. In a 3.3 m box with a 2.50 m room and 0.30 m floor beams, 0.5 m is left for the roof, which is too little. There are two solutions:

- **Solution 1: low floor beams, roof on the box.** The floor beams are reduced to 0.10 m, and the box rests on a concrete slab.
- **Solution 2: high floor beams, separate roof element.** The box keeps 0.30 m floor beams and a thin roof. The roof insulation goes into a separate roof mounted on site, either grid trusses (*gitterspær*, {numref}`fig-grid-truss`) or a flat roof element ({numref}`fig-flat-roof`).

## E1.5 Foundation solutions

:::{figure} figures/e1/floor-roof-solutions.*
:label: fig-floor-roof-solutions
:alt: Two cross-sections of a box side by side. Left, Solution 1: 0.10 m beams with a flexible layer on a concrete slab on EPS, a 2.50 m room and a 0.70 m insulated roof on the box, total box height 3.3 m. Right, Solution 2: box on foundation walls without a slab, 0.30 m insulated floor beams with their underside 0.15 m above the ground and 0.15 m soft insulation below them, a 2.50 m room, a 0.15 m box roof and a separate 15 degree grid truss roof on top, box height 2.95 m.
:width: 100%

The two solutions for the floor and the roof of a box. Red dashed line: assembly interface between the box and the work on site. Dimensions in m.
:::

Sections and foundation details of the two solutions are shown in {numref}`fig-floor-roof-solutions` and {numref}`fig-foundations`.

**Solution 1: concrete slab.** The 0.10 m floor beams only stabilise the box during transport. On site, the box rests on a concrete slab cast on EPS. A flexible layer between beams and slab takes up the tolerance of the slab (3 mm) and dampens impact sound ({numref}`fig-batten-floor`).

**Solution 2: foundation wall without slab.** The box rests on a perimeter wall of lightweight concrete blocks. The floor beams are filled with low-density insulation, with the radon and vapour barrier on top. Soft insulation is added below the beams on site.

Both floors have U ≈ 0.07 W/(m²·K) including the ground, the same as 0.45 m of mineral wool with λ = 0.037 W/(m·K). In Solution 1 the EPS is Sundolitt C60 Climate, λ = 0.033 W/(m·K).

## E1.6 Bathrooms, technical room and ducts

A bathroom has a heavy floor with fall to the drain, more installations than any other room and a lower ceiling than the living rooms. In a box-built house each bathroom is made as a separate pod and placed between the boxes ([Prefabricated bathroom pods](e2-bathroom-pods.md)). One wall of the pod carries all connections and must stay accessible during mounting and for service. The lower ceiling leaves space above the pod for ducts.

Two pods with their connection walls facing each other form a technical room for drains, water, the ventilation unit and the hot water tank ({numref}`fig-pods-tech-room`, {numref}`fig-tech-room-photos`). With the unit in the centre of the house, the ducts are short and the pressure loss is low ({numref}`fig-manifold`). In Solution 2, where the box roof is thin, ducts can run through holes in I-joists ({numref}`fig-ducts-i-joists`).

## E1.8 Facades, windows and roof light

In the example designs, the glazing area is about 20 % of the floor area. Each facade combines fixed windows for daylight and view with insulated venting hatches for ventilative cooling.

Rooms in the middle of the plan get daylight from the roof:

- with a flat roof on the box (Solution 1), from flat-roof windows;
- with a separate pitched roof (Solution 2), from sun tunnels, which can end in an internal 3-layer glazing with diffusing glass flush with the ceiling.

Possible positions in the plans of {numref}`fig-box-layouts` are shown in {numref}`fig-box-layouts-windows`.

:::{admonition} Take aways
:class: tip

1. **The double bed and the truck fix the box width.** The bedroom needs 3.8 m inside; a 4.5 m box with a facade of U < 0.08 W/(m²·K) gives 3.8–3.9 m.
2. **The transport height forces a choice between floor and roof.** A box of 3.3 m with a 2.50 m room leaves 0.8 m; either the floor rests on a slab, or the roof insulation goes into a separate roof element.
3. **A central technical room between the bathroom pods** keeps drains and ducts short and the pressure loss of the ventilation low.
:::
