# Prefabricated bathroom pods

:::{warning} Under revision
This chapter is being reworked and may change substantially.
:::

Per square metre, the bathroom is the most expensive room in a dwelling and the one with the most installations. It contains wet and moist zones, floor drains with fall, hot and cold water, exhaust ventilation, floor heating, electrical installations near water and a water-tight membrane with an often unknown service life. Tiled floors and walls require a stiff substrate with small deflections. On site, the bathroom is built by several trades working one after the other in a small room.

In a prefabricated bathroom pod, this work is done in a factory. The pod is delivered complete, lifted into position and connected to the building at a small number of interfaces. The chapter first describes a bathroom built on site as a reference. It then describes how pods are produced and what the building must provide for them.

## E1.1 On-site construction of bathrooms

{numref}`fig-site-sequence` shows the construction of a bathroom in a timber-frame house built in 2023. The materials and products are current: a profiled steel deck, hydronic floor heating, a water-tight membrane and wall-hung sanitary ware. The method is traditional. The bathroom is built in place, layer by layer, and each trade starts when the previous one has finished.

Three features of this process are relevant for the comparison with pods:

- **Sequential work.** Carpenter, plumber, electrician, concrete worker, membrane installer and tiler work one after the other in the same room. The concrete must dry before the membrane is applied, and the membrane must cure before the tiles are laid. These waiting times add to the construction time.
- **Late detection of errors.** Pipes, drains and cables are covered by the following layers. An error, for example a drain at the wrong height, is often found only after the bathroom is finished, and repair requires the tiles and membrane to be removed.
- **Heavy work on site.** The concrete base for the tiles weighs about 1200 kg. It is cast on the floor structure of the house and carried to the room by hand.

The extent of the membrane follows the zones in {numref}`fig-wet-zones`. The wet zone is the whole floor and the walls near the shower; the other walls are the moist zone. The zones are defined in the Danish wet-room requirements, which apply to pods and site-built bathrooms alike.

## E1.2 Off-site production of bathroom pods

A pod is a bathroom built as one unit with its own floor, walls and ceiling. The membrane, tiles, sanitary fittings, mixers, pipes, cables, lights and, if specified, floor heating are installed in the factory. Kitchens are also produced as pods ({numref}`fig-pod-types`).

Three types of shell construction are common:

- **Light pods** have a steel frame with gypsum fibre boards, for example Badelement's light pod with steel skeleton and fermacell boards {cite:p}`badelement-let`.
- **Concrete-floor pods** have a reinforced concrete floor framed by a steel profile, walls of bent steel cassettes and a ceiling of polyester-coated steel sheet {cite:p}`part-konstruktion`. {numref}`fig-pod-detail` shows such a wall and floor.
- **Concrete pods** are cast in lightweight or normal concrete {cite:p}`bathsystem`.

The weight of the pod determines the required crane capacity and the load on the floor slab. One Norwegian pod weighs about 625 kg per m² of floor {cite:p}`probad2021`, which gives about 3 t for a 5 m² pod.

The materials in a pod are largely the same as in a bathroom built on site. The difference is the production. Pods are produced in series, indoors, and the membrane and tiles are applied in dry conditions by a team that repeats the same tasks. Penetrations of the membrane are sealed by a method that is approved as part of the product, and the pod as a whole can be type-approved by a third party; the Part pod is approved by RISE in Sweden and SINTEF in Norway {cite:p}`part-home`. Before series production starts, a reference pod can be built and inspected against the drawings ({numref}`fig-pod-detail`b).

The pod is wrapped in plastic for transport and storage on site. One manufacturer states a delivery time of 17 weeks from order {cite:p}`part-home`, so the bathroom layout must be fixed early in the design process.

{numref}`fig-pod-cutaway` shows a pod as delivered from the factory, with floor heating, drains, water pipes in the walls and a distribution cabinet on the roof. The connections to the building are collected at a few points on the outside of the pod.

## E1.3 Interfaces between pod and building

A pod is connected to the building at five points: drain, water, exhaust, electricity and, with water-borne floor heating, the heating supply. {numref}`fig-pod-section` shows these connections for a concrete pod placed in a recess in the floor slab next to the installation shaft. The dimensions are taken from the interface document of a Norwegian manufacturer {cite:p}`probad2021`. Other manufacturers use similar values.

:::{figure} figures/e1/pod-section.*
:label: fig-pod-section
:alt: Section drawing of a bathroom pod standing in a recess in the floor slab, with the installation shaft to the right. The pod has a 100 mm floor, walls and a roof, and 2220 mm free height inside. The recess is 110 to 200 mm deep and at least 30 mm wider than the pod all round; the pod rests on neoprene pads and shims, so its floor is level with the dwelling floor. A 75 mm drain runs from the floor drain under the pod into the drain stack in the shaft, and the WC drain of 110 mm enters the stack 260 mm above the floor. Hot and cold water in 20 mm PEX run from a distribution cabinet on the pod roof to the risers in the shaft. An exhaust duct runs from a valve and sleeve in the pod roof, with a bend 170 mm high, to the exhaust duct in the shaft. Cables run from the pod roof to the consumer unit. The slab above is drawn over the pod.
:width: 100%

Section through a concrete bathroom pod and the building around it. The pod floor sits in a recess, so the finished floor is level with the dwelling. Drain, water and exhaust go to the installation shaft, cables to the consumer unit. Dimensions from {cite:t}`probad2021`; pipes not to scale. [View script](https://github.com/CAHVIID/hpbbook/blob/main/chapters/code/e1_pod_section.py)
:::

**Floor recess.** The pod floor is 100 mm thick including tiles, and the floor inside must be level with the floor outside for step-free access. The slab therefore needs a recess of at least 110 mm, and 200 mm if a 75 mm drain runs under the pod to the shaft. The recess is at least 30 mm larger than the pod on all sides. The pod rests on 100 × 100 × 6 mm neoprene pads for impact sound, on shims that take up the unevenness of the slab. The shims are levelled against reference points on the corners of the pod, because an error in level becomes an error in the fall of the floor towards the drain {cite:p}`probad2021`. In a hollow-core or timber deck the recess must be designed together with the structure, so the decision to use pods must be made early.

**Drain.** The floor drain connects through the floor of the pod, the WC through the wall. The WC drain leaves the pod as a 110 mm socket, typically 260 mm above the finished floor. The floor drain needs space under the pod: a 75 mm drain with trap builds 90 mm, a 50 mm pipe 50 mm. This space determines the depth of the recess {cite:p}`probad2021`.

**Water.** Hot and cold water leave a distribution cabinet in the ceiling as 20 mm PEX coils, 2 m long, with shut-off valves on both. The contractor connects them to the risers in the shaft {cite:p}`probad2021`. The pod does not include the water meter or the main stop valve; their position, and access to them, is part of the building design.

**Exhaust.** A sleeve for the exhaust valve is cast into the pod roof. A bend on top of it adds 170 mm to the height, so the pod needs 2220 + 40 + 170 = 2430 mm of space above the finished floor, plus clearance for the lift {cite:p}`probad2021`. The exhaust duct from the bend to the shaft runs above the pod and under the slab or ceiling above.

**Electricity and heating.** Cables come out of the roof in coils of up to 8 m for the consumer unit, typically one circuit for lights, sockets and electric floor heating and one each for the washing machine and the tumble dryer. Water-borne floor heating comes out as 16 mm pipe coils of up to 2 m, for connection to the manifold of the dwelling {cite:p}`probad2021`.

{numref}`fig-pod-drawings` shows two examples of the drawings that define these interfaces. The plan from Badelement gives the floor levels, from 118 mm at the edges down to 83 mm at the shower drain, so the floor falls up to 35 mm towards the drains. The membrane on the walls is shown dashed. The Probad drawing numbers each connection and gives its position from the corner of the pod.

**Installation.** The pods are lifted in by crane with the building, floor by floor, before the slab or roof above closes the space ({numref}`fig-pod-lift`). The installation shaft can be built on site next to the pod, or delivered as part of it. Some pods come with the shaft attached and are lifted in as one piece {cite:p}`badelement-let`. On site, the contractor sets the levels, places the pads and shims, lifts in the pod, checks it for transport damage and water in the drains, and connects it. Until then the pod stays wrapped and closed, and it must not be used as a store room {cite:p}`probad2021`.

## E1.4 Floor heating and energy use in bathrooms

Ceramic tiles and stone draw heat quickly from bare feet. A tiled floor must therefore be kept at about 27 °C to be perceived as comfortable. Bathroom floor heating is often operated as a comfort floor all year, and is often electric. The pod in {numref}`fig-pod-section` can have 120 W/m² of electric floor heating {cite:p}`probad2021`.

:::{figure} figures/e1/tile-diffusivity.jpg
:label: fig-tile-diffusivity
:alt: Line chart of thermal diffusivity in cm²/s, 0.005 to 0.020, against temperature from 20 to 160 °C. Granite has the highest values, falling from about 0.019 to 0.014. Marble and terrazzo start at about 0.010 to 0.011. Several porcelain stoneware tiles (PS, red-S and white-S) lie between about 0.005 and 0.010, all falling slightly with temperature. Vinyl is marked at the bottom left at about 0.002.
:width: 65%

Thermal diffusivity of stone, ceramic tiles and vinyl against temperature. Source unknown.
:::

In {numref}`fig-tile-diffusivity`, granite, marble and ceramic tiles all have a considerably higher thermal diffusivity than vinyl. The sensation at first contact with the foot depends on the thermal effusivity $\sqrt{\lambda \rho c}$, which is also much lower for plastics than for tiles and stone. A plastic or composite floor is therefore perceived as warm at a lower surface temperature. Such floors are durable and have a low mass, and vinyl is approved for wet rooms. Like other wet-room membranes, vinyl will eventually leak.

A warm tile floor affects the energy use in two ways:

- **Winter.** The heat from the bathroom floor alone can cover the total heating demand of a modern apartment if the bathroom door is left open. If the door is closed, the heat goes to the bathroom air and is extracted by the exhaust. This is one explanation for the gap between the calculated and the measured energy use of new dwellings.
- **Summer.** A comfort floor in operation all year adds heat to the dwelling in summer and contributes to overheating, see [Cooling strategy](d2-cooling-strategy.md).

### Worked example: a warm tile floor all year

A pod of the size in {numref}`fig-pod-drawings`c has a floor of 2.49 × 2.00 = 5.0 m². The floor is kept at 27 °C all year, and the bathroom air is at 22 °C. With a combined heat transfer coefficient of about 11 W/(m²·K) from floor to room ([Floor heating](c1-floor-heating.md)), the floor gives

$$
\Phi = h A (\theta_f - \theta_i) = 11 \times 5.0 \times (27 - 22) = 275 \text{ W}
$$

Over a year of 8760 h this is

$$
Q = 275 \times 8760 / 1000 = 2400 \text{ kWh}
$$

A 70 m² apartment built to the passive house level of 15 kWh/m² needs 1050 kWh for space heating in a year. The comfort floor in one bathroom uses more than twice the space heating demand of the whole apartment. With electric floor heating and an electricity price of 2.50 DKK/kWh it costs 6000 DKK a year.

The calculation assumes a constant air temperature of 22 °C. In reality the bathroom air warms up when the door is closed, which reduces the heat from the floor but sends warmer air to the exhaust. A floor that feels warm at 24 °C instead of 27 °C cuts the temperature difference from 5 to 2 K, and the energy use by 60 %.

## E1.5 Time, cost and waste

This section is based on a white paper from the Modular Building Institute, the US trade association for modular construction, written together with five pod manufacturers {cite:p}`mbi2017`. The paper promotes pods, and its figures come from the manufacturers' own projects. They may not be representative of pod projects in general.

**Cost.** The direct cost of a pod is about the same as that of a bathroom built on site, and the capital cost is rarely lower. A comparison must include freight, handling and crane for the pod. According to the paper, cost estimates for site-built bathrooms are often too low.

**Time.** The main advantage is a shorter construction time. A hospital toilet built on site consists of about 800 components installed by a dozen trades over three months. With pods, this work takes place in the factory in parallel with the construction of the building, and the bathrooms are no longer on the critical path. For the Andaz hotel in Tokyo, the pod maker reports 37 % less building time and 44 % fewer man-hours. In Japan, more than 80 % of building projects use prefabricated bathrooms.

**Waste.** The material waste in the factory is below 1.5 %, compared with 7 % or more on a building site.

**Defects.** The paper states that pods have fewer defects, but gives no figures.

According to the paper, pods pay off when:

- the layouts repeat: hotels, hospitals, student housing, care homes and apartments;
- the bathrooms stack, so that one shaft serves the pods above each other;
- the time on site is costly: tight deadlines, bad weather, scarce or expensive craftsmen;
- pods are part of the design from the start, because the structure, the dimensions and the inspections must be adapted to them;
- the trucks and the crane can reach the building.

Pods are less economical in small series, where the benefit of repetition in the factory is small, and in buildings where pods were not planned from the start.
