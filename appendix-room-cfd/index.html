Appendix B: Room CFD model

Under revision

This appendix was not verified, needs rework and will change substantially.

This appendix documents a small 2D computational fluid dynamics (CFD) model of a room section. It is used in Part D to show how the placement of an opening, the supply temperature and the airflow decide whether ventilative cooling gives draught at the feet. The model is made for comparing cases and seeing the physics. It does not give design values.

The model exists in two versions that give the same results:

  • room_cfd.py is the reference version in Python, with plotting functions in room_cfd_plots.py and the figures of this appendix in cfd_examples.py.

  • room-cfd.mjs is the same solver in JavaScript, which runs as the app.

The app¶

Open the room CFD app in a new tab. It runs in your browser and needs no installation. Figure 1 shows it after a run with the default inputs.

The app starts a run when it opens. A run simulates 30 minutes of real time and takes under a minute in a browser. The section view shows the instantaneous field while the simulation runs and the time-averaged field when it finishes. Changing a setting starts a new run.

Under Room you can change the depth of the room from the facade (3–10 m) and the ceiling height (2.4–4 m). A third slider moves the 0.4 m wide block of heat gains along the floor. The exhaust slot stays 0.1–0.2 m below the ceiling, and the cells keep the size set under Mesh. The slider beside the colour bar draws one isoline on the section, for example the 0.15 m/s air speed or the 20 % draught rate. Each of the three views keeps its own value.

The app is a single HTML file, room-cfd.html. Save it anywhere and double-click it to use it offline.

The room and the cases¶

The model is a vertical section through a room 5 m deep and 2.5 m high. The room is 4 m wide, which is only used to turn the airflow and the heat gains into values per metre of width. The supply opening sits in the facade at x = 0 and the exhaust is a 0.1 m slot high in the back wall, at 2.3–2.4 m.

The heat gains (people, equipment and sun-heated surfaces lumped together) are a heat source in a block 0.4 m wide and 1.2 m high in the middle of the room. Their size is set so that the well-mixed room temperature is the same in every case:

Φ=ρ cp qv (Troom−Tsupply)\Phi = \rho \, c_p \, q_v \, (T_{room} - T_{supply})Φ=ρcp​qv​(Troom​−Tsupply​)
(1)

A colder supply then means more cooling, which is what a designer compares. If the gains were kept fixed instead, a colder supply would only lower the whole temperature field and the flow pattern would not change, because buoyancy depends on temperature differences and not on the temperature level.

Turbulence¶

Numerical method¶

Grid. The section is divided into equal rectangular cells, 100 × 50 by default in Python (cells of 5 cm) and 60 × 30 in the app. The grid is staggered (a MAC grid, harlow1965): the pressure and the temperature sit in the cell centres, uuu on the vertical cell faces and vvv on the horizontal faces. This arrangement couples pressure and velocity tightly and avoids the checkerboard pressure oscillations that a collocated grid suffers from.

Time stepping. The model marches in time with the projection method of chorin1968. Each time step has three parts:

  1. Advance the velocity without the pressure gradient, using the advection, diffusion and buoyancy terms. This gives an intermediate velocity u∗\mathbf{u}^*u∗ that does not yet satisfy continuity.

  2. Solve a Poisson equation for the pressure, ∇2p=ρ ∇⋅u∗/Δt\nabla^2 p = \rho \, \nabla \cdot \mathbf{u}^* / \Delta t∇2p=ρ∇⋅u∗/Δt. The matrix depends only on the grid and the boundary types, so it is factorised once before the run (sparse LU in Python, banded Cholesky in JavaScript), and each step only needs a back-substitution.

  3. Correct the velocity with the pressure gradient, un+1=u∗−Δt ∇p/ρ\mathbf{u}^{n+1} = \mathbf{u}^* - \Delta t \, \nabla p / \rhoun+1=u∗−Δt∇p/ρ. The corrected field satisfies continuity to machine precision.

The temperature, and with k-ε model also kkk and ε\varepsilonε, are then advanced with the new velocity. The sink terms of kkk and ε\varepsilonε are treated implicitly so that both stay positive, and kkk and ε\varepsilonε use upwind advection. The time step is set at every step from the stability limits of the explicit scheme:

Δt=min⁡(CΔx∣u∣max,  0.2Δx2νmax,  0.5 s)\Delta t = \min \left( C \frac{\Delta x}{|u|_{max}}, \; 0.2 \frac{\Delta x^2}{\nu_{max}}, \; 0.5 \text{ s} \right)Δt=min(C∣u∣max​Δx​,0.2νmax​Δx2​,0.5 s)
(7)

with the Courant number C=0.25C = 0.25C=0.25 for the TVD scheme and 0.4 for upwind. With 5 cm cells and 0.4 m/s jets this gives time steps of about 0.03 s, so a 30-minute run takes about 50,000 steps.

Why march in time. A steady solver (like SIMPLE in commercial codes) would be faster when the flow has a steady state, but cold air from a window falling into a warm room often flaps and wanders and has none. A time-marching solver handles both cases: it settles if the flow is steady, and it averages if it is not. The fields used for comfort are averaged over the last 10 minutes of the run.

Advection. The advection terms decide how much the solution is smeared out. Two schemes are available.

  • Upwind takes each face value from the cell the flow comes from. It is first order and never oscillates, but it adds numerical diffusion of about ∣u∣Δx/2|u| \Delta x / 2∣u∣Δx/2. In a 0.3 m/s jet on a 5 cm grid that is 0.0075 m²/s, which is larger than the eddy viscosity itself. The results then depend on the grid.

  • TVD (total variation diminishing, the default) adds a limited correction to the upwind value, using the van Leer limiter vanleer1974. Where the field is smooth the scheme is second order, and at peaks and steps it falls back to upwind, so it does not create wiggles. The inlet speed follows from the airflow, the room width and the opening height. A large opening therefore gives a low inlet speed: at 6 air changes per hour a 1.2 m high window gives only 0.02 m/s, and the flow into the room is then driven by buoyancy alone.

Comfort at the feet¶

Draught is evaluated in a simplified occupied zone that starts 0.6 m from the facade and the back wall and reaches 1.8 m up. The model reports the highest mean air speed at ankle height (0.1 m) and two draught indices at that point.

ISO 7730 draught rate ISO, 2005, from fanger1988, with the turbulence intensity set to Tu=40Tu = 40Tu=40 %:

The model was derived from sensations at the back of the neck, so it is conservative at the feet.

Verification¶

Energy balance. At steady state the heat leaving through the exhaust must equal the gains. In all runs the exhaust temperature is within 0.15 K of the well-mixed value.

Continuity. After the projection, the largest divergence in any cell stays at about 10-15 s⁻¹, which is rounding error.

Python and JavaScript. On the same case the two versions agree to 12 significant digits or better, with all three turbulence models.

Grid dependence. The highest floor speed for a 0.1 m slot at 2.3–2.4 m (16 °C supply, 6 ACH, k-ε) on four grids, from cfd_grid.py:

With TVD the speed converges towards about 0.42 m/s, and the default grid is within about 7 % of that. With upwind it keeps rising, because the numerical diffusion shrinks with the cell size. The app uses a coarser grid than Python to keep runs short, so its floor speeds are somewhat lower.

Validation against measurements. The IEA Annex 20 two-dimensional test case Nielsen, 1990 is an isothermal room 9 m long and 3 m high. Air enters through a 0.168 m slot under the ceiling at 0.455 m/s (a Reynolds number of 5000 based on the slot height) and leaves through a 0.48 m opening at the floor of the opposite wall. Laser-Doppler measurements of the mean velocity are given on two vertical lines (x = H and x = 2H) and two horizontal lines, h/2 below the ceiling and h/2 above the floor. The report and the data are in the book’s background/airflow folder, and cfd_annex20.py runs the case on a 321 × 107 grid.

The measured flow is one large eddy that fills the room: the supply air stays on the ceiling as a wall jet almost to the far wall, turns down, and returns along the floor (Figure 2). With k-ε the model reproduces this pattern and the measured profiles (Figure 3). The root-mean-square difference is 0.05–0.06 u0u_0u0​ on three of the lines and 0.12 u0u_0u0​ on the line under the ceiling, where the model lets the jet turn down slightly early. With Chen & Xu the jet leaves the ceiling about halfway into the room and splits the room into three eddies, and the difference is 0.11–0.41 u0u_0u0​. The measured flow is not perfectly two-dimensional: the net flow through the line at x = 2H is about 37 % below the supply flow, so a difference of a few per cent of u0u_0u0​ is as close as a 2D model can get.

Convergence history. The run records the rate of change of uuu, vvv and TTT, the continuity residual, the energy imbalance and two monitor points (Figure 4). With run(case, live=True) the same four panels are drawn in a window while the run goes on.

Example: window or top hatch¶

Figure 5 compares a normal window (sill at 0.9 m, head at 2.1 m) with a top hatch (2.2–2.4 m), both fully open, at 6 air changes per hour. The gains are set for a well-mixed room at 26 °C, and the supply air is 10 K colder, at 16 °C, as on a summer night.

From the window the cold air falls 0.9 m along the facade and runs along the floor. It slows down about 1 m into the room but leaves a cool layer of about 22 °C over the floor (Figure 6).

The flow from the top hatch does not stay on the ceiling. The opening is low and narrow, so the inflow is fast and the Archimedes number is large ((4)), and the jet falls along the facade from 2.2 m. It gains more speed on the way down than the window flow and reaches the floor at up to 0.39 m/s against 0.29 m/s. The fast floor stream also reaches further into the room: the ISO 7730 draught rate is above 20 % up to about 1.6 m from the facade, against about 0.9 m for the window. The air at the floor is 1.5–2 K warmer than with the window, because the falling jet mixes with room air on its way down.

Neither opening meets ISO 7730 near the facade at this supply temperature, while both stay below the ASHRAE 55 ankle limit, at 15 % for the window and 17 % for the hatch. The hatch in the model is a plain slot in the wall. A real bottom-hung hatch deflects the inflow upwards, which helps it attach to the ceiling (D5 Openings for ventilative cooling), so the result is a worst case for the hatch.

Using the Python code¶

A case is a Case object. Every field has a default, so only the differences need to be given:

from room_cfd import Case, Opening, HeatBlock, run, occupied_zone
import room_cfd_plots as plots

c = Case(inlet=Opening("left", 0.9, 2.1), T_in=16.0, ach=6)
c.heat = [HeatBlock(2.3, 2.7, 0.0, 1.2, 1.2 * 1005 * c.q * (26 - c.T_in) / c.W)]
res = run(c, live=True)          # live=True draws the convergence monitor while it runs
print(occupied_zone(res))        # floor speed, DR, TS, PPD_AD and the ankle speed limit
plots.floor_line(res).savefig("floor.png")

run returns a dictionary with the grid (x, y), the averaged fields (u, v, T, speed, nu_t), the inlet speed, the energy check (T_out against T_out_expected) and the convergence history. Each takes one result or a dictionary of labelled results.

Limitations¶

  • 2D. The opening runs the full width of the room. A jet from a slot decays as 1/x1/\sqrt{x}1/x​, slower than a jet from a real window, which decays as 1/x1/x1/x, so speeds far from the opening are overestimated.

  • Inlet only. The supply opening only lets air in. A real wide-open window also lets warm air out at the top, and that two-way flow is not modelled.

  • Turbulence. The standard k-ε model with wall functions reproduces the main flow pattern of a ventilated room, but it is known to be less accurate for flows that separate or are strongly stratified. The comfort indices use a fixed Tu=40Tu = 40Tu=40 % rather than the modelled turbulence.

  • No radiation and adiabatic walls. Surfaces do not exchange heat with the air or with each other, and there is no cold downdraught from glazing.

Use the model to compare openings, temperatures and airflows, and to see why they behave as they do. For design values, use a validated 3D CFD code or measurements.
