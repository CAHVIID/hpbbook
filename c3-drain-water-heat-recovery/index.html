Drain water heat recovery

Under revision

This chapter is being reworked and may change substantially.

Introduction¶

When you take a shower, about 8 L of water at 40 °C leaves the shower head every minute. A few seconds later the same water runs down the drain at 35–38 °C, having lost only a few kelvin on its way through the air and over your skin. At the same moment, 8 L of cold water at about 10 °C enters the house to replace it. A drain water heat recovery unit is a heat exchanger between these two flows. It needs no storage, no pump and no control, because the two flows are there at the same time and stop at the same time. It only works for tappings with that property, and in a home that means showers. A bath is filled first and emptied later, and a kitchen sink or a washbasin draws little water at a time.

Figure 1 shows a vertical unit installed in a basement and how it is connected in a house.

How a unit works¶

Figure 2 shows the two main types.

Vertical units replace about 1.5–2 m of the vertical drain pipe below the shower. The inner pipe is copper. Water falling down a vertical pipe does not fill it, but spreads into a thin film that clings to the wall, so almost all of the copper surface is wetted by a thin, fast-moving layer of warm water. The cold water flows upwards in a coil or a narrow annulus around the pipe, in counterflow to the drain water. This is a good heat exchanger, and the best vertical units recover 70 % of the heat that could at most be recovered. The catch is that a vertical unit needs a storey of straight vertical drain below the shower, which means a basement or a floor below. It suits a house with a basement and an apartment block where the bathrooms are stacked.

Horizontal units sit in the floor, under a shower tray or in a channel drain. They fit a slab-on-ground house and a bathroom renovation. In a channel drain, the heat exchanger is a row of copper tubes along the bottom of the channel, with the cold water flowing inside them against the direction of the drain water. The drain water flows around and over the tubes in a shallow layer. The unit is short, typically 0.7–0.9 m, and the water in the channel moves slowly, so it has less length and less turbulence to give off its heat than the falling film in a vertical pipe. Some manufacturers add turbulators between the tubes to stir the flow. The lower the shower flow, the thinner the water layer around the tubes and the higher the effectiveness. The better horizontal units still reach about 60 %.

Both types must keep the drinking water safe if the wall between the two flows fails. Certified units therefore have a double wall with a vented gap between the drain water and the drinking water, so a leak shows up as water dripping out of the gap rather than as sewage in the drinking water. The drain side gets a film of soap, skin fat and hair over time. A vertical unit is largely self-cleaning, because the falling water scours the wall, while a horizontal unit needs the grate and the channel cleaned as part of normal bathroom cleaning. On the cold side, the unit adds a pressure loss of about 0.1–0.4 bar at shower flow.

Effectiveness¶

How good a unit is, is expressed by its effectiveness: the heat it actually transfers, divided by the most heat that could be transferred.

The most that could be transferred is the heat that would cool the drain water all the way down to the cold water temperature, or warm the cold water all the way up to the drain water temperature, whichever flow has the smaller heat capacity rate C=ρ V˙ cpC = \rho\,\dot V\,c_pC=ρV˙cp​.

The ε-NTU method is used when the two flows are not equal.

Connecting the unit¶

The preheated water can go to three places (Figure 3):

  1. To the cold side of the shower mixer only. The water heater is still fed with cold mains water.

  2. To the water heater only. The shower mixer is still fed with cold mains water.

  3. To both. All the water that ends up in the shower has passed through the unit, so the flow on the cold side equals the flow down the drain. This is called equal flow or balanced connection.

Power of the water heater¶

Connection 2 feeds the preheated water to the cold inlet of the one central water heater, a tank or a district heating heat exchanger. It needs a single pipe from the unit to the heater, not a heater at each tap.

During a shower, the water heater only has to supply the heat that the unit does not recover (Table 3).

The heater must still be sized for the shower without the unit, 16.6 kW, for two reasons. The unit only preheats once warm water reaches the drain, so at the start of every shower the heater gets cold water. All other taps, and a bath, get no preheat at all. The unit therefore lowers the energy but not the installed power. That matters for an instantaneous electric water heater, which at these powers needs a three-phase supply. A tank or a district heating heat exchanger covers the peak anyway.

Effectiveness increases with lower shower flow¶

A unit with ε=0.70\varepsilon = 0.70ε=0.70 has NTU=0.70/0.30=2.3NTU = 0.70/0.30 = 2.3NTU=0.70/0.30=2.3, and one with ε=0.41\varepsilon = 0.41ε=0.41 has NTU=0.7NTU = 0.7NTU=0.7. When the shower flow rises, CCC rises in proportion. UAUAUA also rises, because faster water transfers heat better, but less than in proportion. So NTUNTUNTU falls, and the effectiveness with it. Figure 4 (left) shows this for the two units of Table 1, assuming UAUAUA grows with the square root of the flow. A low-flow shower head therefore saves twice: it uses less hot water, and the unit recovers a larger share of the heat in the water that is used.

Figure 4 (right) shows the saving for all three connections over the range of certified effectiveness. The balanced connection is always best. For units with a low effectiveness, connection 2 (to the water heater) is slightly better than connection 1; for the best units it is the other way round. In practice the choice is often made by the pipe layout. Connection 1 needs only a short pipe from the unit to the shower mixer, and connection 2 needs a pipe from the unit to the water heater, which may be far away. Connection 3 needs both, and it is the one to aim for in a new building.

How much does it save?¶

The annual saving is the shower heat times the saving fraction:

Qsaved=s QshowerQ_{saved} = s\,Q_{shower}Qsaved​=sQshower​
(10)

where QshowerQ_{shower}Qshower​ is the heat for showers over the year, Q=ρ V cp (θmix−θc)Q = \rho\,V\,c_p\,(\theta_{mix} - \theta_c)Q=ρVcp​(θmix​−θc​) summed over all showers. Showers are the largest single use of hot water in most homes, typically half to two-thirds of the hot water heat. One 8-minute shower at 8 L/min and 40 °C takes 2.2 kWh (Table 1). A person who showers on three days out of four uses about 600 kWh a year for showers, and a balanced unit with ε=0.5\varepsilon = 0.5ε=0.5 saves 45 % of that, about 270 kWh per person per year.

The saving varies over the year with the cold water temperature. With cold water at 5 °C in late winter, the unit with ε=0.5\varepsilon = 0.5ε=0.5 warms it by 16 K; at 15 °C in late summer, by 11 K. The fraction saved hardly changes (equation eq-dwhr-saving gives 46 % and 44 %), because the shower also needs more heat in winter.

What the saved heat is worth depends on how the water is heated. With an electric resistance heater, every kWh of heat saved is a kWh of electricity saved. With a heat pump that delivers hot water at a COP of 3, it is only 0.33 kWh of electricity. Drain water heat recovery therefore pays back about three times faster in a house with an electric water heater than in a heat pump house. In a heat pump house, it competes with the heat pump: both are ways of not buying the same kWh of heat.

Drain water heat recovery also helps when the tank is used as a thermal battery, as in Domestic hot water. It cuts the heat drawn from the tank during each shower, by about half with a good balanced unit, so the tank needs to store less heat to cover the evening showers from a charge in cheap hours. A smaller tank also has a smaller standing loss.

Worked example: channel drain or vertical pipe?¶

A family of four in a 150 m² house takes three showers a day, the same as in the tank sizing example in Domestic hot water. Each shower lasts 8 minutes at 8 L/min and 40 °C, and the cold water is 10 °C on average. They are rebuilding the bathroom and consider two options:

  • A. A Joulia-Inline 5 channel drain (ε=0.41\varepsilon = 0.41ε=0.41), connected to the shower mixer only, because the water heater is on the other side of the house (connection 1).

  • B. A Showersave QB1-21XE vertical unit (ε=0.70\varepsilon = 0.70ε=0.70) in the basement below the bathroom, connected to both the mixer and the water heater (connection 3).
