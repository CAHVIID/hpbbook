Domestic hot water

Under revision

This chapter is being reworked and may change substantially.

Introduction¶

For most of the twentieth century, domestic hot water was a small item in the heat budget of a Danish house. A typical older house with energy label D needs around 150 kWh of space heating per m² of floor area per year. Hot water adds 13 kWh/m², less than a tenth of the total.

Better houses have changed the picture (Figure 1). Insulation, airtightness and heat recovery ventilation have cut the space heating demand of a passive house to 15 kWh/m², and of a house in the Danish low-energy class to about 10 kWh/m². The hot water demand has not changed, because it depends on the people living in the house and not on its envelope. A family takes the same showers in a passive house as in a house from 1965. In a passive house hot water is therefore close to half of the heat demand, and in a low-energy class house it is more than half.

Hot water in Building Code

The Danish energy frame calculation (Be18) uses a fixed hot water demand of 250 L per m² of heated floor area per year, heated from 10 °C to 55 °C Aggerholm, 2018. With the average properties of water between 10 °C and 55 °C this is

Q=ρˉ V cˉp (θ−θc)=994⋅0.250⋅4.182⋅(55−10)3600=13.0 kWh/(m2 year)Q = \bar\rho\,V\,\bar c_p\,(\theta - \theta_c) = \frac{994 \cdot 0.250 \cdot 4.182 \cdot (55 - 10)}{3600} = 13.0 \text{ kWh/(m}^2\,\text{year)}Q=ρˉ​Vcˉp​(θ−θc​)=3600994⋅0.250⋅4.182⋅(55−10)​=13.0 kWh/(m2year)
(1)

For a 150 m² house it amounts to 2000 kWh per year, or about 100 L of 55 °C water per day which is a reasonable figure for a family of three or four.

However, measured hot water use varies by a factor of two or three between households of the same size, and it depends much more on the number of occupants, their age and their habits than on the floor area.

How much hot water?¶

Energy in a tapping¶

The heat needed to warm a mass mmm of water from the cold water temperature θc\theta_cθc​ to the temperature θ\thetaθ at the tap is

Q=m cp (θ−θc)Q = m\,c_p\,(\theta - \theta_c)Q=mcp​(θ−θc​)
(2)

where m=ρ Vm = \rho\,Vm=ρV. Both the density ρ\rhoρ and the specific heat capacity cpc_pcp​ of water vary with temperature. Between 10 °C and 55 °C the density falls from 999.7 to 985.7 kg/m³, while cpc_pcp​ stays close to 4.18 kJ/(kg·K). For hot water calculations we use the averages over that range, ρˉ=994\bar\rho = 994ρˉ​=994 kg/m³ and cˉp=4.182\bar c_p = 4.182cˉp​=4.182 kJ/(kg·K). One litre of water warmed by 1 K then takes 4.16 kJ, or 1.15 Wh. The cold water from Danish waterworks is about 8–10 °C as a yearly average, with a seasonal swing from around 5 °C in late winter to 15 °C in late summer.

At the tap, the user mixes hot water from the tank with cold water to get the temperature they want, typically 38–40 °C for a shower and 45 °C for washing up. The fraction of hot water in the mix follows from an energy balance:

fh=θmix−θcθh−θcf_h = \frac{\theta_{mix} - \theta_c}{\theta_h - \theta_c}fh​=θh​−θc​θmix​−θc​​
(3)

With the tank at 55 °C and cold water at 10 °C, a 40 °C shower is two-thirds hot water and one-third cold. Equation (2) gives the same energy whether it is applied to the mixed water at 40 °C or to the hot water fraction at 55 °C, so either can be used. What matters is to be clear which one a consumption figure refers to. Water use statistics are usually given at the tap, while calculation standards usually give litres at 55 °C or 60 °C.

Power when tapping¶

The energy per day is modest. The power during a tapping is not. The heat flow needed to warm water flowing at a volume flow V˙\dot VV˙ is

Φ=ρ V˙ cp (θ−θc)\Phi = \rho\,\dot V\,c_p\,(\theta - \theta_c)Φ=ρV˙cp​(θ−θc​)
(4)

Table 1 shows the energy and the power for some typical tappings. A shower at 8 L/min needs about 17 kW while it runs, and filling a bath needs about 25 kW.

Figure 2 shows a day of tappings for a family of four. Each tapping lasts only minutes, but a shower draws 15 kW while it runs. The whole day adds up to only 5.7 kWh. The tank simulator in Appendix E: Stratified tank simulator contains more tapping profiles: the EN 16147 test cycles, the statistical IEA SHC Task 26 profiles, profiles measured in Danish houses and a few illustrative ones, each with its source.

These powers are far larger than anything else in the heating system of a low-energy house. The whole house may need only 2–3 kW of space heating on the coldest day, and a heat pump sized for that cannot heat a shower as it runs. There are two ways out:

  • Instantaneous heating. District heating can deliver 30–40 kW through a heat exchanger in a flat station, so the water is heated as it is used and nothing is stored.

  • Storage. A heat pump or a small boiler heats a tank slowly, over hours, and the tank delivers the high power during the tapping. The tank decouples the power the heat source must deliver from the power the user draws.

Rules of thumb

  • The cold water temperature is on average 10 C, which is ok for most calculations, but actually fluctuates between 8-17 C throughout the year.

  • 1 L of water warmed by 1 K takes about 1.15 Wh. 100 L warmed by 45 K takes about 5 kWh.

  • One 8-minute shower uses about 60 L of 40 °C water, which is 40 L of 55 °C water and about 2 kWh of heat.

  • Hot water use is about 30–40 L of 55 °C water per person per day, or about 550–750 kWh per person per year before losses.

  • The Be18 calculation value is 13 kWh/m² per year, about 2000 kWh per year for a 150 m² house.

  • A shower draws about 15–20 kW while it runs, several times the design heat load of a low-energy house.

Hot water heaters¶

Four types of hot water heater are common in Danish single-family houses. They differ in where the heat comes from and in what else the unit does.

Electric water heater. A tank with an electric heating element and a thermostat. Each kWh of hot water costs one kWh of electricity, plus the standing loss of the tank, which is set by the insulation and the tank temperature. The electric water heater is the reference for the other solutions in this book.

Example: Metro Therm water heaters

The range runs from 14 to 288 L, insulated with polyurethane foam, with a thermostat that can be set up to about 65 °C Metro Therm, 2026.

Hot water heat pump. A tank with a small air-to-water heat pump on top. The heat pump takes its air through ducts, either outdoor air or exhaust air from the house. The COP at an air temperature of 20 °C is typically around 3 to 4. With outdoor air, the COP falls in winter, when the air is cold. With exhaust air, the heat source is close to room temperature all year, but the air flow is limited by the ventilation.

Example: MetroAir Aqua

The MetroAir Aqua heats a tank of 190 or 260 L with 1.6 kW of heat pump capacity. The COP is 3.7 and 4.1 at an air temperature of 20 °C for the two tank sizes Nathan, 2025.

Heat pump for space heating and hot water. An air-to-water or ground source heat pump that serves both the floor heating and a hot water tank, often built into the indoor unit. The heat pump switches between floor heating at about 35 °C and charging the tank at 50–55 °C. The COP is lower when it heats hot water than when it heats the floor, because the condensing temperature is higher. The share of the year’s heat that goes to hot water grows as the space heating demand falls, so in a low-energy house the COP for hot water matters as much as the SCOP for space heating.

Example: Kronoterm Adapt 2

An air-to-water heat pump with an outdoor unit of 2 to 18 kW and the natural refrigerant R290. The SCOP is up to 6.22 at a flow temperature of 35 °C (EN 14825, average climate), and the flow temperature can reach 75 °C Kronoterm, 2026. With the HYDRO C2 indoor unit, the hot water tank is built into the indoor unit.

Compact unit. A compact unit combines balanced ventilation with heat recovery, an exhaust-air heat pump and a hot water tank in one cabinet. The heat pump takes heat from the exhaust air after the heat exchanger and uses it for the hot water and, in some units, the supply air. The heat source is limited by the ventilation air flow, so the unit suits houses with a small heating demand, such as passive houses. Because ventilation, heat pump and tank share one cabinet, the parts are harder to reach and replace than in separate units, and a fault in one part can take ventilation and hot water out of service together.

Example: Nilan Compact P

The Compact P has a 180 L tank in a cabinet of 900 × 610 × 2065 mm. The counterflow heat exchanger has a temperature efficiency of up to 95 % Nilan, 2026. The unit is certified as a Passive House component at air flows of 92 m³/h and 172 m³/h.

The Danish Energy Agency keeps an official list of heat pumps sold in Denmark, Varmepumpelisten, with the tested efficiency of each product Energistyrelsen, 2026. It is the place to compare heat pumps of the types above.

Standing loss of the tank¶

A hot water tank loses heat to the room around it all the time, whether or not water is drawn. This standing loss is set by the insulation and by the temperature difference between the tank and the room.   • Year: 1.43 kWh a day, or 520 kWh a year. In a row house of 144 m², that is 3.6 kWh/m², or more than a quarter of the 13 kWh/m² for the hot water itself.

The example gives an upper limit for a tank in use. Every tapping replaces hot water with cold water at the bottom of the tank, so the mean tank temperature, and with it the loss, is lower than in the example. The tank simulator in Appendix E: Stratified tank simulator shows that the night-charged tank in the tank sizing example below loses less than half of the loss for a tank held at the set temperature. A tank placed inside the heated building also gives its loss to the room. In the heating season, this covers part of the space heating, and in summer it adds to the internal heat gains. Over a year, a tank of class A+ instead of C saves about 320 kWh of heat.

Electricity prices over the day¶

A household on an hourly contract pays the spot price of the hour plus a set of fixed and time-dependent charges. In eastern Denmark (DK2) in 2026, the price per kWh is

p(t)=(pspot(t)+pnet(t)+pTSO+ptax) (1+0.25)p(t) = \big(p_{spot}(t) + p_{net}(t) + p_{TSO} + p_{tax}\big)\,(1 + 0.25)p(t)=(pspot​(t)+pnet​(t)+pTSO​+ptax​)(1+0.25)
(7)

where pspotp_{spot}pspot​ is the day-ahead price of the hour, pnetp_{net}pnet​ the network tariff of the local grid company, pTSOp_{TSO}pTSO​ Energinet’s system and network tariffs (7.2 + 4.3 øre/kWh Energinet, 2025), ptaxp_{tax}ptax​ the electricity tax and 0.25 the VAT. The electricity tax was 72.7 øre/kWh until 2025. In 2026 and 2027 it is cut to the EU minimum of 0.8 øre/kWh Skatteministeriet, 2025, which makes the hourly part of the price far more important than before.

Two of the terms change over the day (Figure 4):

  • The spot price follows the balance between demand and wind and solar production. On a typical weekday it has one peak in the morning and a higher one at 17–20, when people come home and cook and solar production falls away. It is lowest at night and, on sunny days, at midday.

  • The network tariff is a fixed time-of-use tariff. Radius charges, in winter (October to March), 11 øre/kWh at 00–06, 32 øre/kWh at 06–17 and 21–24, and 96 øre/kWh at the peak 17–21. In summer, the peak tariff is less than half of that Bolius, 2026.

The two peaks coincide. On the day in Figure 4, the consumer price runs from 1.17 DKK/kWh at 03:00 to 4.68 DKK/kWh at 19:00, a factor of four. The family of four draws 57 % of its hot water heat at 06–08 and 37 % at 17–21, in the most expensive hours of the day. Heated as it is used, its hot water costs on average 2.38 DKK/kWh of electricity. This is the price weighted by the hot water heat drawn in each hour, and it is higher than the daily average of 1.89 DKK/kWh. Heated at night, 00–06, it costs 1.22 DKK/kWh, half as much.

The saving from charging at night depends on the heat source. With an electric water heater, the family uses 5.7 kWh of electricity a day for hot water, and charging at 00–06 instead of at the time of use reduces the cost by 6.6 DKK on this day. With a heat pump at a COP of 2.5, the reduction is 2.6 DKK a day. Both values are upper limits, since they are based on a single day with a large price spread, neglect the standing loss, and assume a heat source that can deliver the shower power directly. Over a year, the saving is of the order of a few thousand DKK with a resistance heater and about a thousand DKK with a heat pump.

The primary energy factor and CO₂ over the day¶

The Danish energy frame (BR18) uses one fixed primary energy factor for electricity, 1.9. The real factor changes from hour to hour with the production mix. Each source iii gets a non-renewable primary energy factor, and the factor of the mix in hour ttt is

fP,nren(t)=∑iEi(t) fP,nren,i∑iEi(t)f_{P,nren}(t) = \frac{\sum_i E_i(t)\, f_{P,nren,i}}{\sum_i E_i(t)}fP,nren​(t)=∑i​Ei​(t)∑i​Ei​(t)fP,nren,i​​
(8)

where Ei(t)E_i(t)Ei​(t) is the electricity from source iii in hour ttt. Wind and solar have a non-renewable factor of zero. Fossil plants have a factor close to 1/η1/\eta1/η. The CO₂ intensity behaves the same way, and for this reason it serves as a proxy for CO₂ in this book.

Danish electricity emitted on average 124 g CO₂-eq/kWh in 2025, counted on consumption and including imports. Single hours ranged from 36 to 363 g/kWh. In winter, the average day goes from 130 g/kWh at 01:00 to 166 g/kWh at 16:00, and in summer from 81 to 100 g/kWh Electricity Maps, 2026. Price and CO₂ often point the same way, because the expensive evening hours are also the hours with more fossil production and imports. But they are not the same signal. A windy afternoon can be cheap and clean while the night after it is calm and dirty. A charging strategy that follows the price is therefore not automatically the one with the lowest CO₂.

For hand calculations, the hourly data can be reduced to a few periods of the day. Scale the BR18 factor by the mean CO₂ intensity ccc of each period, relative to the daily mean cˉ\bar ccˉ, weighted by the length of each period:

fP(t)=1.9 c(t)cˉf_{P}(t) = 1.9\,\frac{c(t)}{\bar c}fP​(t)=1.9cˉc(t)​
(9)

Figure 5 shows the result for Denmark in 2021, in four periods of the day. The night, 22–06, gets a factor of 1.66, the day, 06–17, 2.05, and the evening, 17–22, 1.95. Averaged over the 24 hours, the factor is 1.9. The spread is only about ±10 % around 1.9, much smaller than the spread in price, which is why a charging strategy saves far more money than primary energy.

Sizing the tank as a thermal battery¶

A hot water tank is a battery for heat. When it is charged, the heat source runs. When hot water is drawn, the tank delivers. If the tank is big enough, the two no longer have to happen at the same time, and the heat pump can run in the hours when electricity is cheap or has a low primary energy factor. The question is how big “big enough” is.

The heat a fully charged tank holds, counted from the cold water temperature, is

Qtank=ρˉ V cˉp (θset−θc)Q_{tank} = \bar\rho\,V\,\bar c_p\,(\theta_{set} - \theta_c)Qtank​=ρˉ​Vcˉp​(θset​−θc​)
(10)

Not all of it can be used. Between the hot water at the top and the cold water at the bottom there is a mixing zone, the thermocline, and water from that zone is too cold for a shower. The tank is “empty” for the user long before all of it is at θc\theta_cθc​. We account for this with a usable fraction ηuse\eta_{use}ηuse​, typically 0.8–0.9 for a well-stratified tank and lower for a tank that mixes when it is charged.

Sizing by hand in four steps¶

The method compares, hour by hour, the heat the tank has to deliver with the heat the charging strategy puts in. It is the same method used to size water reservoirs, and it needs nothing more than a spreadsheet with one row per hour.

Step 1. Hourly heat demand. From the tapping profile, add up the heat drawn in each hour, Q=ρ V cp (θ−θc)Q = \rho\,V\,c_p\,(\theta - \theta_c)Q=ρVcp​(θ−θc​), using the volume and use temperature of each tapping. Add the standing loss of the tank as a constant amount per hour, Φloss=UA (θset−θroom)\Phi_{loss} = UA\,(\theta_{set} - \theta_{room})Φloss​=UA(θset​−θroom​). The sum over the day is the heat the tank must receive, QdayQ_{day}Qday​.

Step 2. Hourly charging. Spread QdayQ_{day}Qday​ evenly over the hours the charging strategy allows. With NNN allowed hours, the heat source must deliver Φch=Qday/N\Phi_{ch} = Q_{day}/NΦch​=Qday​/N during those hours. This is the first check of the design: if Φch\Phi_{ch}Φch​ is larger than the heat output of the heat pump, the strategy cannot work, whatever the tank size.

Step 3. Cumulative curves. Add up the demand and the charging hour by hour, and plot both cumulative curves over the day. The difference between them, charge minus demand, is the change in the heat stored in the tank since midnight. The tank must never hold less than nothing, so lift the charge curve until it just touches the demand curve from above. The largest vertical gap between the two curves is then the heat the tank must store, QstoreQ_{store}Qstore​. In a spreadsheet, this is simply the maximum minus the minimum of the column “cumulative charge − cumulative demand”.

Step 4. Volume. Solve equation (10) for the volume, with the usable fraction:

V=Qstoreρˉ cˉp (θset−θc) ηuseV = \frac{Q_{store}}{\bar\rho\,\bar c_p\,(\theta_{set} - \theta_c)\,\eta_{use}}V=ρˉ​cˉp​(θset​−θc​)ηuse​Qstore​​
(11)

Round up to the next standard tank size.

The method is a first estimate. It assumes the tank is either hot or cold, with a fixed usable fraction, and that the heat source charges evenly over its allowed hours. In a real tank, the thermocline grows while the tank sits, the heat pump is switched by a thermostat at a sensor partway up the tank, and the standing loss depends on how much of the tank is hot. The tank simulator in Appendix E: Stratified tank simulator handles all of this, so the hand estimate should always be checked there.

Worked example: night charging for a family of four¶

A family of four uses the profile “Family of 4, morning and evening” from the tank simulator (Appendix E: Stratified tank simulator): two showers at 06:30–07:00, one at 20:00, and small tappings during the day. Three showers a day, rather than one per person, matches the Be18 calculation value for a 150 m² house; measured Danish households shower even less Marszal-Pomianowska & others, 2021. The tank is kept at 55 °C, the cold water is 10 °C, and the tank has UA=1.2UA = 1.2UA=1.2 W/K in a room at 20 °C. How large a tank is needed if the heat pump may run only between 00:00 and 06:00? How large is it with uniform charging over the whole day?

Rules of thumb

  • 1 kWh stored at 55 °C, counted from 10 °C cold water, needs about 20 L of water, or 23 L with a usable fraction of 0.85.

  • Charging only at night means storing a full day of hot water: about 40–45 L per person.

  • Charging whenever needed, with a thermostat, needs about 20–25 L per person, enough to cover the largest peak.

  • The heat pump must deliver at least the daily heat divided by the number of allowed hours, plus a margin.

Circulation in apartment buildings¶

In an apartment building, hot water is produced centrally, in a district heating substation with a tank or a plate heat exchanger, and distributed to the flats through risers. Without circulation, the water in a riser cools between tappings, and the user has to run the tap until hot water arrives. A circulation loop prevents this. A pump keeps the hot water moving through the supply pipes and back to the substation, so that the whole network stays hot at all hours. The return of the loop is kept above 50 °C to limit the growth of Legionella.

The heat lost from the circulation pipes is

Φcirc=∑jLj (θw,j−θa,j)Rj′\Phi_{circ} = \sum_j \frac{L_j\,(\theta_{w,j} - \theta_{a,j})}{R'_j}Φcirc​=j∑​Rj′​Lj​(θw,j​−θa,j​)​
(13)

where LjL_jLj​ is the length of pipe section jjj, θw,j\theta_{w,j}θw,j​ the water temperature, θa,j\theta_{a,j}θa,j​ the surrounding air temperature and Rj′R'_jRj′​ the thermal resistance per metre of insulated pipe. The loss runs 8760 hours a year, whether hot water is used or not.

Measurements show that the loss is large. In Danish apartment buildings, circulation losses were 23–70 % of the heat delivered to the hot water system, and 54–89 % in office buildings Bøhm, 2013. In 134 Swedish multi-family buildings, the measured loss ranged from 0.5 to 76 kWh per m² of heated floor area per year. The mean was 14 kWh/m² in older buildings and 6 kWh/m² in newer ones, against an assumed 4 kWh/m² Burke et al., 2020. Figure 7 shows these results together with the earlier Swedish studies that Burke et al. review. All the mean values lie above the design assumption.

Figure 8 adds these mean losses to Figure 1. In a building of the Danish low-energy class, the circulation loss of 6 kWh/m² is 21 % of the net heat demand and 60 % of the space heating demand. Hot water and circulation together are two-thirds of the heat demand.

Part of the loss is useful in the heating season, because the pipes run inside the heated building. In summer, it adds to the internal heat gains.
