D5 Openings for ventilative cooling

Under revision

This chapter is being reworked and may change substantially.

D5.1 Introduction¶

Bottom-hung (tilt) windows are common in new Danish dwellings. They are suited to airing in winter: the opening is small, secure and protected from rain, and the sash directs the cold inflow upwards. In summer the small opening area and a driving temperature difference of only a few kelvin limit the airflow. Jiang et al. report that bottom-hung windows may not provide sufficient ventilation in the warmer months for this reason Jiang et al., 2025.

A side-hung window opens over its full height and, at an opening angle of 60° or more, over its full width. For single-sided ventilation at a small ΔT, the airflow is four to five times that of a tilted sash of the same size. The inflow is slow and drops to the floor, but in summer it is only slightly cooler than the room air and therefore provides cooling rather than draught. The bottom-hung window is thus suited to winter airing and the side-hung window to summer cooling.

This chapter covers the free area of the common opening types, the airflow through them at a small temperature difference, the flow pattern of the inflow, the resulting draught at the feet, and venting hatches. It builds on the orifice and stack equations in D4 Ventilative cooling.

D5.2 Opening types and their free area¶

The airflow is determined by the smallest cross-section the air passes through, the free opening area, and not by the glass area.

Side-hung (hinge at the side, opening angle α): the free edge leaves a vertical gap over the full sash height h:

s=2wsin⁡(α/2),A=min⁡(s,w) hs = 2w\sin(\alpha/2), \qquad A = \min(s, w)\,hs=2wsin(α/2),A=min(s,w)h
(1)

where w is the sash width. For α ≥ 60° the gap equals the sash width, and the free area equals the full sash area.

Bottom-hung (hinge at the bottom, stroke s at the top): the gaps are two triangles at the sides that taper to zero at the hinge, plus a slot at the top:

A=s w+2⋅12s h=s (w+h)A = s\,w + 2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} s\,h = s\,(w + h)A=sw+2⋅21​sh=s(w+h)
(2)

Tilt fittings limit s to about 100–150 mm.

Top-hung (hinge at the top, opening outwards at the bottom): the same geometry turned upside down, so A = s(w + h) as well. The widest gap is at the bottom, where the inflow enters, and the outflow passes through the narrow gaps at the top.

Venting hatches are openings made only for ventilation, usually without glass.

D5.3 Discharge coefficient of windows and louvres¶

The orifice equation (3) contains the product CdAC_d ACd​A, and a discharge coefficient is only meaningful together with the area it refers to. For windows, CdC_dCd​ refers to the free opening area of D5.2. For a sharp-edged opening it is about 0.6. The discharge coefficient of a window is not a constant: it varies with opening area, window type and temperature difference, and for a side-hung window it decreases at large ΔT Heiselberg et al., 2000Heiselberg et al., 2001. Cd=0.6C_d = 0.6Cd​=0.6 is used as design value for windows in summer conditions.

Louvres. Weather louvres and ventilation grilles are tested to EN 13030 CEN, 2001. The discharge coefficient is determined from the measured airflow and pressure drop and refers to the core area, the opening inside the frame with the blades removed. It therefore already includes the obstruction of the blades, and must not be multiplied by the free area between the blades. The product CdAcoreC_d A_\text{core}Cd​Acore​ is called the aerodynamic free area. EN 13030 classifies louvres by the discharge coefficient: class 1 ≥ 0.4, class 2 0.3–0.4, class 3 0.2–0.3 and class 4 < 0.2.

Consequence for design. For the same airflow, an opening with Cd=0.2C_d = 0.2Cd​=0.2 requires three times the area of an open window with Cd=0.6C_d = 0.6Cd​=0.6. A venting hatch of 0.5 m² behind a grille of Cd=0.2C_d = 0.2Cd​=0.2 is equivalent to an open window of only 0.17 m². When a louvre and a window act in series, they are combined with the general form of (4), and the louvre dominates.

D5.4 Airflow at small ΔT¶

For a single opening, warm room air leaves through the upper part and cool outdoor air enters through the lower part. The pressure difference increases linearly with the distance from the neutral plane, so the orifice equation is integrated over the height of the gap. For a gap of constant width, such as that of the side-hung window, the neutral plane is at mid-height and the integration gives the single-opening equation of D4 Ventilative cooling:

Qst=CdA3g h ΔTTQ_{st} = \frac{C_d A}{3}\sqrt{\frac{g\,h\,\Delta T}{T}}Qst​=3Cd​A​TghΔT​​
(3)

For the bottom-hung window the gap width varies with height, and the integral is evaluated numerically (script). Most of the free area is in the top slot and the upper parts of the side triangles. The neutral plane is therefore at about 1.1 m of the 1.3 m sash height, and the inflow passes through the narrow lower parts of the triangles. The airflow per square metre of free area is lower than for the side-hung window.

D5.5 Flow pattern of the inflow¶

The airflow rate determines the cooling power, while the flow pattern determines whether the inflow is perceived as air movement, as draught at the ankles, or not at all. Heiselberg, Svidt and Nielsen showed in laboratory tests that the flow from an open window can be described by jet theory and stratified-flow theory, and developed simple flow-element models for the velocities in the occupied zone Heiselberg et al., 2001.

The balance between buoyancy and momentum in the inflow is measured by the Archimedes number:

Ar=g ΔT a0T U02Ar = \frac{g\,\Delta T\,\sqrt{a_0}}{T\,U_0^2}Ar=TU02​gΔTa0​​​
(4)

where a₀ is the effective inflow area (C_d times the inflow part of A) and U₀ = q₀/a₀ is the inflow velocity. At small Ar the inflow behaves as a jet and keeps its direction; at large Ar it is governed by buoyancy and falls.

(a) Side-hung window: floor flow. The side-hung window opens over its full height, and the inflow passes slowly through the lower half. It follows from (3) that U₀² is proportional to ΔT in purely stack-driven flow, so Ar is about 1 independent of ΔT. The inflow therefore falls towards the floor and spreads in a semi-radial layer in summer as well as in winter; only the temperature and velocity of the layer change. Nielsen et al. measured this for a side-hung window (0.81 × 1.38 m, sill 1.0 m) and found that the maximum velocity near the floor follows the same form as for a displacement diffuser Nielsen et al., 2000:

u=Kw q0x+x0u = K_w\,\frac{q_0}{x + x_0}u=Kw​x+x0​q0​​
(5)

K_w lies between about 4 and 20 m⁻¹ and depends on Ar’ and the opening area. x₀ is a virtual origin of 0 to −3 m. The maximum velocity occurs at ankle height. In summer the layer is a few kelvin below room temperature and provides cooling. In cold weather the same layer causes draught, and the side-hung window must be restricted or closed. Strong wind adds momentum and can carry the inflow further into the room before it drops.

(b) Bottom-hung window placed high: ceiling jet. A bottom-hung window is normally placed high, as a top light or a hatch. The inward-tilted sash deflects the inflow upwards, where it attaches to the ceiling as a wall jet Svidt et al., 2000:

um=Ka U0 a0x+x0,Ka≈7,  x0≈−2 mu_m = K_a\,U_0\,\frac{\sqrt{a_0}}{x + x_0}, \qquad K_a \approx 7,\; x_0 \approx -2\ \text{m}um​=Ka​U0​x+x0​a0​​​,Ka​≈7,x0​≈−2 m
(6)

The jet entrains warm room air along the ceiling and is mixed and warmed before it reaches the occupied zone. For this reason the high bottom-hung window is suited to winter airing.

(c) Bottom-hung window at large ΔT: separation. A cold jet remains attached to the ceiling only up to a penetration depth x_s, which decreases with increasing Ar. Beyond x_s it separates and falls into the occupied zone. At a large ΔT and a small driving pressure (0.2–0.4 Pa), it drops close to the window and continues along the floor Heiselberg, 2006. For a slot at the ceiling of a 3 m high room, model tests show that the cold jet leaves the ceiling before it reaches the end wall once Ar = βghΔT₀/u₀² ≥ 0.02, with h the slot height. In that geometry this is a supply temperature only 0.7 K below the return Nielsen, 1990. In cold weather the bottom-hung window must therefore be opened only slightly, so that the jet remains attached to the ceiling.

D5.6 Draught at the feet¶

The floor stream from a low or full-height opening (Figure 3a) reaches the occupants at ankle height. Whether it is perceived as cooling or as draught depends on its velocity and temperature and on the thermal sensation of the occupant. Two models are in use.

ISO 7730. The draught rate predicts the percentage dissatisfied with draught ISO, 2005:

DR=(34−ta)(v−0.05)0.62(0.37 v Tu+3.14)DR = (34 - t_a)(v - 0.05)^{0.62}(0.37\,v\,T_u + 3.14)DR=(34−ta​)(v−0.05)0.62(0.37vTu​+3.14)
(9)

with tat_ata​ the local air temperature (°C), vvv the mean air speed (m/s) and TuT_uTu​ the turbulence intensity (%). The usual limit is DR < 20 %. The model was developed for people at neutral thermal sensation, with air at 20–26 °C and below 0.5 m/s at the neck. At the feet it is conservative, and it does not apply to occupants who are warm and prefer air movement (D6 Ceiling fans).

ASHRAE 55 ankle draught. Liu et al. measured the response to air movement at the ankles as a function of the whole-body thermal sensation TS (−3 to +3) Liu et al., 2017. The model, adopted in ASHRAE 55, gives

PPDAD=100 ez1+ez,z=−2.58+3.05 vankle−1.06 TSPPD_{AD} = 100\,\frac{e^z}{1 + e^z}, \qquad z = -2.58 + 3.05\,v_{ankle} - 1.06\,TSPPDAD​=1001+ezez​,z=−2.58+3.05vankle​−1.06TS
(10)

with vanklev_{ankle}vankle​ the air speed at 0.1 m. The limit PPDADPPD_{AD}PPDAD​ < 20 % corresponds to vanklev_{ankle}vankle​ < 0.39 + 0.35·TS m/s: 0.39 m/s for a neutral occupant and 0.22 m/s for a slightly cool occupant (TS = −0.5). Warm occupants accept higher velocities.

Measurements. Bugenings et al. measured the flow from a full-scale low louvre in a test room at a small temperature difference: the inflow was 24–25 °C and the room 0.4–1.9 K warmer Bugenings et al., 2025. The maximum velocity was at 0.1 m above the floor in all cases, also without buoyancy. Calculated from their published data, the ISO 7730 draught rate in the louvre’s path passed 20 % at about 7 air changes per hour (0.31–0.36 m/s), while the ankle model stayed below 20 % in every case.

Fitzner and Finke measured the flow from six tilted bottom-hung windows in a 180 m³ office in winter Fitzner & Finke, 2012. In the hour before the velocity measurements, the airflow was 3.3 air changes per hour at a mean temperature difference of 13.8 K, a cooling power of 2.7 kW. With the radiator below the window switched off, the inflow fell to the floor, and the velocity at 0.1 m was 0.21–0.27 m/s with DR of 27–37 %.
