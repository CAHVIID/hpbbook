D3 Façade design for daylight and low cooling demand

Under revision

This chapter is being reworked and may change substantially.

D3.1 Introduction¶

The heating demand of the course row house has been reduced to a very low level. The same envelope retains solar gains in summer, and in the 2050 climate the cooling demand becomes the governing design criterion. The façade determines the solar gain and is therefore the first step of the cooling strategy (D2 Cooling strategy). Solar gain that is not admitted through the façade does not have to be removed by ventilative cooling (D4 Ventilative cooling) or offset by ceiling fans (D6 Ceiling fans).

Daylight and solar gain are transmitted through the same glazing, and a smaller window, a lower g-value or a deeper overhang reduces both. BR18 allows daylight to be documented by climate-based simulation to EN 17037 in the form of the metric sDA300. This metric can be used as a constraint when facades are optimized.

D3.2 Solar gains through the façade¶

The solar gain through a window is

Φsol=g Fsh Ag I⊥\Phi_{sol} = g \, F_{sh} \, A_g \, I_{\perp}Φsol​=gFsh​Ag​I⊥​
(1)

where ggg is the total solar energy transmittance of the glazing (–), FshF_{sh}Fsh​ the shading factor of overhangs and fins (–, 1 without shading), AgA_gAg​ the glazed area (m²) and I⊥I_{\perp}I⊥​ the solar irradiance on the façade (W/m²). The glazing properties ggg, UgU_gUg​ and τv\tau_vτv​ are defined in a3-glazing.md.

The ggg-value given in product data applies at normal incidence. It decreases with the angle of incidence, slowly up to about 50° and steeply above 60°, because the reflection at each pane increases. Simulation tools such as IDA ICE account for this; hand calculations with the normal-incidence value overestimate the gain when the sun strikes the glass at a large angle.

D3.3 Fixed shading: overhangs and side fins¶

The fixed shading elements considered are an overhang above the window and side fins at its edges (Figure 2).

An overhang shades according to the profile angle Ω\OmegaΩ, which is the solar altitude projected onto the vertical plane perpendicular to the façade:

(2)

where α\alphaα is the solar altitude and γ\gammaγ the angle between the sun’s azimuth and the façade normal. An overhang of depth ddd placed at the window head shades a window of height HHH completely when

dtan⁡Ω≥H.d \tan \Omega \geq H .dtanΩ≥H.
(3)

At 55.7 °N the noon altitude is 90°−55.7°+δ90° - 55.7° + \delta90°−55.7°+δ: 58° at the summer solstice, 34° at the equinoxes and 11° at the winter solstice (Figure 3a). Towards south the noon sun is high in summer and low in winter, so an overhang blocks the summer sun and admits the winter sun. A 1.0 m overhang over a 1.1 m high, 2.3 m wide south window shades the entire window at noon when Ω>47.7°\Omega > 47.7°Ω>47.7°, i.e. from late April to mid-August. The overhang has the same length as the window, so in the morning and afternoon the sun reaches the glass past its ends. The overhang reduces the annual direct radiation on the window from 420 to 162 kWh/m² (−61 %) and the radiation from May to August by 85 % (164 to 24 kWh/m²). From November to February the reduction is 22 % (65 to 51 kWh/m²), which increases the heating demand (Figure 3b).

On east and west façades the irradiance is highest when the sun is low and γ\gammaγ is close to zero, so Ω≈α\Omega \approx \alphaΩ≈α is small and an overhang has little effect. Fixed elements block sun at near-normal incidence poorly. Side fins are effective only on narrow windows, as shown below, and movable shading or a lower g-value is normally required. Side fins shade according to the horizontal angle γ\gammaγ. Towards south they block the low morning and evening sun, which reaches the glass at grazing incidence; towards north they block the low summer sun from north-east and north-west.

A fin of depth ddd on each side of a window of width WWW shades a strip of width dtan⁡γd \tan \gammadtanγ on the side facing the sun, and the window is fully shaded when dtan⁡γ≥Wd \tan \gamma \geq Wdtanγ≥W. Towards south the effect is small (Figure 4). For the 2.3 m wide south window, 0.5 m fins on both sides reduce the direct radiation from May to August by 23 % (164 to 125 kWh/m²) and the annual radiation by 19 %, compared with 85 % in summer for the 1.0 m overhang. The fins shade only in the morning and afternoon. Around noon, when the gain is highest, γ\gammaγ is small and the fins provide almost no shading.

On east and west façades only the fin on the south side of the window is effective, because the sun is on the north side only early in the morning and late in the evening (Figure 5). The irradiance on an east façade is highest in the early morning, when the sun is low and γ\gammaγ is small. A single 0.5 m fin on a 2.3 m window reduces the direct radiation from May to August by 10 % (east 161 to 145, west 172 to 155 kWh/m²).

The shaded strip dtan⁡γd \tan \gammadtanγ is a larger fraction of a narrow window. Dividing the 2.3 m window into two 1.15 m windows, each with a fin, keeps the glass area and doubles the shaded fraction. The reduction from May to August increases to 18 % on the east façade (132 kWh/m²) and 17 % on the west façade (142 kWh/m²), and the annual reduction from 15 % to 26 %. Sun at near-normal incidence is still not blocked. Closely spaced vertical louvres apply the same principle, and movable shading also blocks the sun at near-normal incidence.

Fixed shading also obstructs part of the sky and reduces the diffuse daylight throughout the year. When sDA300 is a constraint, this usually limits the overhang depth.

Movable shading (external blinds, screens, shutters) can block more of the sun when required and leave the window unobstructed on overcast days. It requires control, either automatic or by the occupants. This chapter considers fixed elements only; shutters for heat waves belong to a later step of the cooling strategy.

Rules of thumb

  • Towards south at 55–56 °N, an overhang at the window head with depth d≈Hd \approx Hd≈H shades the window completely around noon from late April to mid-August.

  • Overhangs are effective towards south. Towards east and west, fins are effective only on narrow windows; use movable shading or a lower g-value.

D3.4 Fixed shading: solar-coatings¶

A solar-control coating on the outer pane (surface 2) reflects most of the near-infrared part of the solar spectrum, which carries about half of the solar energy but no visible light. The coating reduces ggg more than τv\tau_vτv​, and the selectivity

S=τvgS = \frac{\tau_v}{g}S=gτv​​
(4)

increases. Clear triple glazing has S≈1.4S \approx 1.4S≈1.4–1.5. Solar-control coatings reach S≈2S \approx 2S≈2, which is close to the theoretical limit: a glazing that transmits all visible light and blocks all infrared radiation still transmits about half of the solar energy.

Replacing the reference glazing with Suncool 66/33 reduces ggg by 35 % and τv\tau_vτv​ by 17 % (Table 1). Below τv≈0.5\tau_v \approx 0.5τv​≈0.5 the room appears dark from the inside, and a larger window is needed to meet sDA300, which increases the solar gain again.

A coating also changes the colour of the transmitted daylight. In a Danish pilot study, 18 subjects assessed the daylight in a scale model of an office behind six glazings (Figure 6) Dubois & Johnsen (2003). Some subjects found the colour unacceptable at τv\tau_vτv​ of 0.66–0.70, and most subjects at 0.50. The ratings did not follow τv\tau_vτv​ alone: the reference glazing at 0.77 was rated higher than a coated glazing at 0.79.

CIBSE guideline LG10 (CAH: source needs to be checked) states that humans percieve glass with LT > 60% as visually neutral or “clear”. When transmittance drops below approx. 50, occupants begin to percieve the room as noticeable dim or tinted, which can trigger additional electric lighting.

Rules of thumb

  • In Copenhagen, 1 m² of south glazing with g=0.49g = 0.49g=0.49 admits about 180 kWh of solar heat from May to August; with g=0.32g = 0.32g=0.32, about 120 kWh.

  • Selectivity above 2 is not available; keep τv≥0.6\tau_v \geq 0.6τv​≥0.6.

D3.5 Daylight requirement¶

BR18 requires daylight in every habitable room. For unobstructed rooms BR18 accepts a glass area of at least 10 % of the floor area, increased when the light transmittance is below 75%.

However, compliance can be documented by climate-based daylight simulation according to EN 17037: an illuminance of at least 300 lx on at least 50 % of a reference plane 0.85/0.5 m above the floor for at least 50 % of the daylight hours,

sDA300,50%≥50 %.\mathrm{sDA}_{300,50\%} \geq 50\ \% .sDA300,50%​≥50 %.
(5)

The working plan is 0.85 m in offices, and 0.5 m in residences. The default reflectances are 0.2 for floor, 0.7 for ceiling, all else 0.5.

sDA300 increases with window area and head height, light transmittance and a southern orientation, and decreases with overhangs, fins and external obstructions.

D3.6 Multi-objective optimisation¶

Given the sheer number of design options: different window sizes, glazing types, overhang depths, orientations etc, we use multi-objective optimization. For instance, given seven window sizes, five glazings, five overhang depths, four fin depths and two orientations, you have 7 × 5 × 5 × 4 × 2 = 1,400 combinations, each requiring an energy and a daylight simulation. A full factorial study of this size is impractical, and most of the runs are far from a useful design, anyway.

The problem is stated as

min⁡x  (QC(x),  QH(x))subject tosDA300(x)≥50 %\min_{x} \; \big( Q_C(x),\; Q_H(x) \big) \quad \text{subject to} \quad \mathrm{sDA}_{300}(x) \geq 50\ \%xmin​(QC​(x),QH​(x))subject tosDA300​(x)≥50 %
(6)

where xxx is a combination of the design variables, and QCQ_CQC​ and QHQ_HQH​ are the annual cooling and heating demand (kWh/m²) of the zone.

Cooling and heating demand are objectives to be minimised. Daylight is a constraint that must be satisfied.

The two objectives conflict, so there is no single optimal design. A design is Pareto-optimal if no other feasible design is better in one objective without being worse in the other. The Pareto-optimal designs form the Pareto front (Figure 7). A design that is not on the front can be improved in one objective without a penalty in the other, whereas the choice between designs on the front is a trade-off.

One method is to convert both demands to the electricity use of the actual systems, QH/COP+QC/EERQ_H/\mathrm{COP} + Q_C/\mathrm{EER}QH​/COP+QC​/EER, and select the design on the front with the lowest total.

The infeasible designs lie below the front: small windows and glazings with low τv\tau_vτv​ give a lower cooling demand but do not meet the daylight constraint.

The optimisation in IDA ICE (AutoMOO in the parametric runs) uses the role assigned to each output, objective or constraint, to direct new runs towards the feasible part of the pareto front. A few hundred runs therefore resolve the front better than the same number distributed over a full grid. The drawback is that trends away from the front are not resolved.

Normalisation¶

Energy, daylight and comfort have different units, so to depict the trends on a graph, each output is therefore divided by a normalizing reference value:

The four quantities are dimensionless and can be shown in one figure per orientation. Figure 8 depicts the expected trends.

Expected trends¶

  • Daylight increases steeply at small WFR and then levels off, because additional glazing mainly raises the illuminance in parts of the plane that already exceed 300 lx. The WFR at which nD=1n_D = 1nD​=1 is the minimum window for the given glazing and shading.

  • Cooling increases almost linearly with WFR, because the solar gain is proportional to the glazed area. The slope is determined by the g-value and, towards south, by the overhang.

  • Heating changes little. Towards south it has a shallow minimum where the additional solar gain balances the additional heat loss. Towards north it increases with WFR.

  • Comfort. hIIh_{II}hII​ remains close to zero up to the WFR at which the free-running zone first exceeds the category II limit, and then increases faster than the cooling demand.

Solar-control glazing and overhangs lower the daylight and cooling curves together and therefore shift the minimum WFR to larger values. Whether a larger window with solar-control glazing performs better than a smaller window with the reference glazing is determined by comparing nCn_CnC​ at the two minimum-WFR points.
