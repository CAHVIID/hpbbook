D1 Future climate and weather files

Under revision

This chapter is being reworked and may change substantially.

D1.1 Introduction¶

Future climate changes and given the life span of buildings, they need to be designed for that. This chapter gives an overview of the climate challenges.

D1.2 What is in a weather file¶

A weather file holds one year of hourly outdoor conditions for one location: dry-bulb temperature, humidity, direct normal, diffuse and global horizontal radiation, wind speed and direction, and cloud cover. Three abbreviations recur:

  • TMY, typical meteorological year: the general term for one artificial year assembled from twelve typical months taken from many years of data. It represents average weather, not extremes. The data behind it can be measurements or, as in the Annex 80 files (section D1.4), climate model output.

  • DRY, design reference year: the Danish national typical year, published by DMI (section D1.3). It is assembled in the same way, but only from months measured at one station and selected with the method of ISO 15927-4, and it is the year used to show compliance with the Danish building regulations. A TMY from another source for the same location therefore differs from the DRY in both the data and the selection method.

Weather files come in many different file formats. The best known is EPW, the EnergyPlus weather file, which is a text format with 8 header lines followed by 8,760 hourly rows. Most building simulation tools read it.

The DRY files can be downloaded as EPW or CSV from DMI.

D1.3 The Danish design reference year - DMI¶

A reference year is one artificial year of hourly weather. It is assembled from twelve real calendar months, each picked from a longer measured record as the most typical month of its kind. Danish Meteorological Institute (DMI) selects the months with the Finkelstein–Schafer method of ISO 15927-4. The method compares the distribution of daily values in each candidate month with the distribution for all years, using temperature, global radiation and humidity as primary parameters and wind and precipitation as secondary ones.

Denmark has two generations of design reference year (DRY), both for the DMI station at Sjælsmark north of Copenhagen, with solar radiation measured at DTU in Lyngby:

  • DRY 2001–2010, the reference used in building regulations until now.

  • DRY 2011–2023, the 2025 reference year DMI, 2025. Its twelve months come from 2014–2023.

Future reference years¶

DMI has also published climate-projected reference years for three emission scenarios:

  • RCP2.6

  • RCP4.5

  • RCP8.5

RCP is Representative Concentration Pathway and reflect future greenhouse gas concentrations in the atmosphere: Representative Concentration Pathway

Each RCP scenario was projected for four periods:

  • 2035–2054

  • 2045–2064

  • 2055–2074

  • 2080–2099

DMI considers RCP4.5 the most likely scenario, as it corresponds to about 2.7 °C of global warming by the end of the century DMI, 2025.

Extra

Climate models give only daily temperature and precipitation, not the hourly radiation and humidity a simulation needs. So DMI used the projected daily values to choose months from the measured 2014–2023 record: for each calendar month the measured month whose temperature distribution best matches the projection is picked. Where two months match equally well, the one that also matches the projected precipitation best is chosen. Every hour in a future file is therefore real, measured weather from the last decade.

Figure 1 shows the current and future DRY files, and Table 1 summarises them.

Figure 2 shows how the hourly temperatures of each file are distributed in the winter and the summer half-year. The winter curve moves to the right between the two present-day files, with the median jumping from 1.7 °C to 4.5 °C, and then hardly moves. The cold tail below −8 °C is gone after 2001–2010. The summer curve moves steadily, with the median rising from 14.0 °C to 15.4 °C, and its warm tail grows past 26 °C.

Enthalpy is the annual mean total specific enthalpy of the outdoor air per kg dry air, h=1.006 T+x (2501+1.86 T)h = 1.006\,T + x\,(2501 + 1.86\,T)h=1.006T+x(2501+1.86T) in kJ/kg, with the humidity ratio xxx found from air temperature, relative humidity and pressure.

Enthalpy expresses the total energy content of the atmosphere (latent and sensible heat). In a building context, it indicates the increased work load on a chiller that needs to both cool the air, as well as shed the water content.

The UTCI column is explained in the section on heat stress indices below.

Take-aways

Four take-aways:

  1. A big step has already happened. Going from the 2001–2010 to the 2011–2023 reference year raises the mean temperature by about 1.5 °C and cuts the hours below 0 °C by more than half, yet severe frost still occurs.

  2. Summers get warmer, but slowly. Hours above 26 °C rise from 37 today to 80–90 around mid-century.

  3. Radiation and humidity hardly change. Global radiation stays at about 1,020–1,040 kWh/m² and mean relative humidity at 81–83 %. Solar gains in the future files are those of today.

  4. The projections flatten out. The 2080–2099 file is no warmer than 2055–2074; the two differ only in January, May and October. Because every future month must be a measured month from 2014–2023, the files can never be hotter than the hottest month of that decade.

Heat stress index UTCI¶

Air temperature alone does not tell how hot it feels outdoors. Sun, wind and humidity matter as much, and a heat stress index combines them into one number.

The Universal Thermal Climate Index (UTCI) Bröde et al., 2012 is the index used in this chapter. It is the air temperature of a reference environment that gives the same physiological strain as the actual one, for a person walking at 4 km/h. The reference environment has 50 % relative humidity, almost no wind and no radiation beyond that of the air temperature. The inputs are air temperature, humidity, wind speed and mean radiant temperature. Here the mean radiant temperature is that of a person standing in the open, found from the direct and diffuse solar radiation in the weather file (the SolarCal method of ASHRAE 55). The tables count the hours with UTCI above 26 °C, which is where moderate heat stress begins.

UTCI assessment scale

UTCI is an outdoor index. Its ten stress classes Bröde et al., 2012 are:

UTCI (°C)Stress class
above 46extreme heat stress
38 to 46very strong heat stress
32 to 38strong heat stress
26 to 32moderate heat stress
9 to 26no thermal stress
0 to 9slight cold stress
−13 to 0moderate cold stress
−27 to −13strong cold stress
−40 to −27very strong cold stress
below −40extreme cold stress

Two alternatives are common:

  • PET, the Physiological Equivalent Temperature Höppe, 1999, is built on the same idea as UTCI and uses the same inputs, but with an indoor reference environment and a simpler body model. Moderate heat stress starts at about 29 °C Matzarakis et al., 1999.

  • WBGT, the wet-bulb globe temperature, is a weighted mean of the natural wet-bulb, globe and air temperatures. It is the index of occupational and sports heat limits. It needs measured wet-bulb and globe temperatures, so from a weather file it can only be estimated Australian Bureau of Meteorology, n.d..

D1.4 Future weather files - Annex 80¶

The IEA EBC Annex 80 project made a set of Copenhagen files Machard et al., 2024 from the MPI-REMO regional climate model (EURO-CORDEX): typical meteorological years (TMY) for:

  • 2001–2020 (representing 2010)

  • 2041–2060 (representing 2050)

  • 2081–2100 (representing 2090)

and three heat-wave years picked from 2041–2060 under RCP8.5 and bias-adjusted against observations from 2001–2019.

Unlike the DRY files, these are model output, not measured months, so they can be hotter than anything seen so far. Figure 3 shows them and Table 2 summarises them in the same way as the DRY files.

In the typical years the Annex 80 files warm in both seasons (Figure 4): the winter median rises from 4.2 °C to 6.2 °C and the summer median from 14.1 °C to 15.8 °C. The heat-wave years have almost the same summer median as the typical year of the same period. They differ in the warm tail, which is longest in the most severe year, with 192 hours above 26 °C against 82 in the typical year.

Note

*The heat-wave file “longest” (2045) has no solar radiation from 1 to 14 August: global, direct and diffuse radiation are zero in all 336 hours, while temperature and humidity run on normally. The gap lies inside the heat wave the file was made for (15 July to 20 August 2045). Its annual global radiation is therefore about 90 kWh/m² too low, and its UTCI hours are underestimated. Use this file for temperature and humidity only, and the “most intense” or “most severe” file where solar gains matter. The three heat-wave files also contain 4–6 corrupt hours of global and diffuse radiation (values far above 1,400 W/m²); these were removed and interpolated before the table was computed.

D1.5 Extremes¶

Heat waves¶

Heat waves raise mortality, mainly through cardiovascular and respiratory strain, and the old and the sick are most at risk. The hot summer of 2018 caused about 380 additional deaths in Finland, and the heat-related mortality risk in Helsinki was about 2.5 times that of the surrounding rural areas Velashjerdi Farahani et al., 2021.

The summer of 2026 gave similar numbers in western Europe. All three are provisional estimates from the national health agencies. In France, 5,764 more people than expected died during the heat wave of 17 June to 2 July, an excess of 36 % Santé publique France, 2026. In England, 2,877 deaths were associated with two hot spells in May and June UK Health Security Agency, 2026. In Spain, more than 5,200 deaths were attributed to heat between May and August, the highest number since monitoring began in 2015 Meteored, 2026.

A reference year is assembled from typical months, so it contains no real heat wave. The Annex 80 project therefore produced separate heat-wave years.

They are the three years of 2041–2060 with the longest, the most intense (hottest) and the most severe (longest and hottest combined) heat wave. Table 3 compares them with the typical years.

Warm nights are the real problem. Night ventilation removes heat in proportion to the airflow times the temperature difference between indoor and outdoor air (D4 Ventilative cooling). When the night stays above 20 °C, that difference is only a few kelvin, so the thermal mass is not emptied and the next day starts warmer. After a few such days the building has no reserve left.

Simulations of Finnish apartment buildings without cooling show the result Velashjerdi Farahani et al., 2021. The heat wave of 2018 had 24 days above 25 °C and 5 tropical nights, and gave about 10,000 degree-hours above 27 °C indoors.

The same heat wave moved to the climate of 2050 has 15 tropical nights and gives about 14,000 degree-hours above 27 °C, and the degree-hours above 32 °C, a health risk threshold, triple from about 1,000 to 3,000. Openable windows were the most effective passive measure, but only a cooling unit removed the overheating completely.

The heat wave of 2018¶

The summer of 2018 was a Nordic heat-wave summer. To see how it compares, July and August of DRY 2011–2023 were replaced by DMI observations from Sjælsmark (station 06188), 1 July to 31 August 2018.

How the file was built

  • Hourly mean air temperature, relative humidity, pressure, global radiation, wind, cloud cover and precipitation from the DMI open data API. Dew point is computed from temperature and humidity.

  • DMI measures only global radiation. Direct and diffuse radiation are split from it with the Erbs model Erbs et al., 1982. On the original July and August of the DRY, the split gives about 10 % less direct and 5 % more diffuse radiation than DMI’s own values.

  • The jumps in temperature and humidity at 1 July and 1 September are spread over 13 hours. All other months are the original DRY.

Download the EPW. Table 4 and Figure 6 compare it with the Annex 80 files on the same indicators as Table 2 and Table 3.

The 2018 heat wave was on par with the Annex 80 “most intense” year 2055. Their summers have almost the same mean (18.2 and 18.0 °C), the same 99th percentile (29.2 and 29.1 °C) and about the same degree-hours above 26 °C (217 and 194 Kh). It was far from the “most severe” year 2054, which has nearly twice the degree-hours (399 Kh), 192 instead of 125 hours above 26 °C and 42 instead of 29 days above 25 °C. The summer of 2018 is therefore a realistic heat wave for the 2050s, but not the worst case.

Urban heat island¶

The urban heat island intensity is the air temperature difference ΔTUHI=Turban−Trural\Delta T_\mathrm{UHI} = T_\mathrm{urban} - T_\mathrm{rural}ΔTUHI​=Turban​−Trural​. It is mainly a night-time effect and peaks a few hours before sunrise on calm, clear summer nights. The causes:

  • Heat storage: brick, concrete and asphalt store solar heat during the day and release it at night.

  • Low sky view factor: street canyons see little of the cold night sky, so long-wave cooling is reduced.

  • Little evaporation: sealed surfaces drain rain away instead of evaporating it.

  • Low wind speed: buildings reduce convective cooling and mixing.

  • Waste heat: traffic, buildings and air-conditioning condensers.

In Denmark the effect is modest but not negligible. Figure 7 shows it for the four largest Danish cities. On the 3 % warmest days of the year, Copenhagen is on average 1.5–2 K warmer than its rural surroundings at night, and on some nights close to 3 K. The difference almost disappears around 09:00. The other three cities stay at about 1 K Sørup & others, 2026. In four Finnish cities the mean intensity is 0.5–1.2 K, peaking at about 1.5 K around 05:00 in Helsinki Taylor & others, 2025.

For comparison, a model study of 100 European cities at 100 m resolution for 2008–2017 finds an average heat island indicator of 1.43 °C. Green areas and unsealed soil already lower it by 0.45 °C on average, and more of both could lower it by about another 0.5 °C Lauwaet et al., 2024.

The heat island acts exactly when night ventilation works. Figure 8 colours every hour by the temperature difference available for ventilative cooling, ΔT=Tin−Tout\Delta T = T_\mathrm{in} - T_\mathrm{out}ΔT=Tin​−Tout​, with Tin=24T_\mathrm{in} = 24Tin​=24 °C. The heat island is set to 2 K at night, the upper end of the Copenhagen averages.

The effect of urban heat islands is more pronounced with future climate, but the magnitude will probably remain the same or even decrease a little as global radiation drops, and mitigating actions are implemented like more green areas in the built environment. Cities are projected to warm by about 1.9 K by 2100 under an intermediate emission scenario Zhao et al., 2021, while in Brussels the summer night-time heat island stays at about 3.1–3.2 K up to the 2060s under both RCP4.5 and RCP8.5 Lauwaet et al., 2016.
